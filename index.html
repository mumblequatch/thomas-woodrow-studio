An audio drama studio,
built with Claude.

One continuous signal path — no manual seams

I set out to make audio drama and immediately hit friction. The tools didn't fit the way I wanted to work, and the gap between the writing, the recording, the voice production, and the release was full of manual seams. So I started closing the gaps. Working with Claude, that turned into something larger than I planned: a suite of tools that lets one person write, record, produce, and ship serialized audio drama without ever leaving the way I actually think.

What follows is both halves of that — the studio I built to remove the friction, and the work it exists to make. The collaboration with Claude runs through all of it, and it's been productive enough, across enough different kinds of problem, that I thought it was worth showing you.

Working Method

The thing that changed wasn't that I got faster. It's that friction became fixable.

Every creator accumulates a list of inefficiencies they've learned to live with — the export that takes six manual steps, the format that won't talk to the next tool, the thing you do by hand every time because automating it was never worth the week it would cost. You route around them. They become invisible, and they quietly set the ceiling on what one person can make.

Collaborating with Claude collapsed the cost of fixing them. When I noticed a seam, I could close it that afternoon, not someday. And the effect compounded in a way I didn't anticipate: fix enough friction points in a row and you're no longer patching a workflow, you've built a new one. The production pipeline here didn't come from a design document. It came from solving the next inefficiency, then the next, until the write → record → produce → ship path had no manual seams left in it. The innovation was emergent — the byproduct of never again having to say "that's just how it is."

The division of labor settled into something consistent. On the tooling, Claude worked as a pair-programmer I directed: I set the architecture and the constraints, it wrote to them and flagged where things drifted. Lathe carries that relationship in its own history — a Co-Authored-By: Claude trailer on 55 of 62 commits, and a CLAUDE.md defining what it could and couldn't decide. On the creative work I kept Claude on a shorter leash and wrote the leash down: the Underworld story bible opens with a dated "Rules for Claude" casting it as archivist, not author — preserve my text as canon, no autonomous synthesis. Different jobs, same principle. I decide what's true; Claude helps me build toward it fast enough that the building never blocks the making.

ACT I

The Studio

The tools built to remove the friction. Local-first, private, running on my own machine — in daily use.

LatheA native writing app for structurally complex work

Beta · In daily use

A macOS desktop app for long-form writing where structure matters — screenplays, serialized scripts, novels. Two-pane outline beside a real editor, with Fountain syntax classification, live decorations, typewriter scrolling, and atomic file I/O.

It reads and writes Fountain, txt, md, rtf, and docx, and it hooks directly into the production pipeline: "Apply Conformed Changes" folds machine-generated edits back into the open script. It exists because nothing off the shelf let me hold an outline and a draft in the same view the way I think about them.

Stack  Tauri 2.10 · Rust · Svelte 5 · TypeScript · CodeMirror 6 Scale  ~12,700 LOC · 62 commits over three months · 55 co-authored with Claude

The production pipelineWrite to master, no manual seams

Functional · In production use

The piece I'm proudest of, because no one designed it top-down — it accreted from solving one inefficiency at a time until the whole path was automated. The master Fountain script is the single source of truth; everything downstream is regenerable from it.

The pipeline parses the script, force-aligns it against a recorded take, splits the audio into per-character REAPER tracks, runs AI voice conversion and cleanup, masters through Auphonic, and lays up the mix. What used to be a day of manual editing between "recorded" and "produced" is now a chain of hotkeys.

The engineering runs deeper than the surface suggests. The character-split engine is a real package with a maintained test suite, not a script. Auphonic mastering runs as a live background daemon. The ElevenLabs integration is a full voice-library manager, not a one-off API call. Underneath sit local ML stages — Whisper for transcription, Montreal Forced Aligner for alignment, pyannote for diarization — and hardware control down to the RME interface over OSC.

Stack  Python · Shell · REAPER Lua · ElevenLabs · Auphonic · Whisper / MFA / pyannote Signal  a ~1,900-LOC pytest suite on the character-split core — the strongest maturity marker in the studio

The publishing stackDesktop to distributed, by hotkey

In active use

The last seam was getting finished work out the door. This stack ships written, audio, and video content to the web from the desktop: Cloudflare R2/CDN with edge purging, GitHub Pages, a Ghost newsletter with an embedded audio player generated straight from markdown + audio, and a podcast host — each wired to a keystroke, each self-logging every URL it publishes.

ACT II

The Work

What the studio exists to make. Original serialized audio drama and short-form work.

Underworld Original serialized audio drama · the flagship

In development · partially produced

A planned three seasons of roughly twenty-minute episodes: a seven-year-old sorcerer follows his abducted parents into the Underworld, a land of the dead running on a memory-erasing drug economy. I pitch it as His Dark Materials meets the first time you saw Star Wars.

It's not just a script — it's a realized world. An Obsidian story bible of ~78,000 active words holds character bios, world lore, a thematic spine, and craft notes on the sound signatures and the magic system. Episode one is written and its season architecture is locked. There's a produced trailer with per-character voices, a composer pitch deck built for Mark Korven, and a full business plan for the venture. This is also where the Claude collaboration is most deliberately governed.

Built  ~78,000-word story bible · trailer produced · Korven pitch deck · business plan Method  governed by a dated "Rules for Claude" archivist contract
